Clean Energy: Powering Tomorrow

For more than two hundred years, humanity has depended on coal, oil and natural gas to run industries, warm houses and power transport. These fossil fuels made rapid industrial growth possible, but they come with a heavy cost. When they are burned, huge quantities of carbon dioxide are released into the atmosphere, and this gas traps heat and slowly warms the planet. Higher temperatures are connected to melting ice, more violent storms, longer dry periods and rising oceans. For this reason, governments, scientists and businesses are now searching for cleaner ways to produce the energy that modern life depends on.

Renewable energy comes from natural sources that are replaced faster than we use them. Sunlight, wind, moving water, underground heat and plant material are the main examples. These sources will not run out in any time frame that matters to human beings, and most of them produce little or no pollution while they are generating electricity.

Solar power is the fastest growing renewable source in the world today. Solar panels are made of photovoltaic cells, usually built from silicon, which convert sunlight directly into electricity. When light hits the cell, electrons are freed from their atoms, and this movement of electrons is an electric current. Over the last ten years the cost of solar panels has dropped dramatically, so ordinary families can now afford to put panels on their roofs and get their money back in a short time. Large solar farms in deserts can supply power to entire cities. The main weakness of solar energy is that it only works when the sun is shining, so output drops at night and on cloudy days.

In my own city, the local council recently covered the roofs of several government schools with solar panels. The teachers say that electricity bills have gone down noticeably, and the students have started a club that measures how much power the panels produce every month. Projects like this show that renewable energy is not only for big companies but can also help ordinary communities.

Wind power uses the movement of air to turn the blades of a turbine. The blades are connected to a shaft that spins a generator, and the generator produces electricity. Today's turbines are giant structures, sometimes higher than a skyscraper, and one turbine is able to supply electricity to thousands of households. Wind farms are built on open plains, along coastlines and increasingly out at sea, where the wind is stronger and more steady. Offshore projects cost a lot to construct and repair, but they deliver a big and dependable supply of power. Some people object to wind turbines because of the noise they make and the way they change the appearance of the landscape.

Water has been used to generate electricity for longer than any other renewable source. Engineers construct a dam on a river to store a large lake of water behind it. When the water is released, it rushes through tunnels and spins turbines at the base of the dam. These plants can start and stop within minutes, so they are very helpful when demand suddenly rises. On the other hand, dams flood wide areas, push people out of their homes and disturb the river, which damages fish and other animals.

Tidal energy is a newer idea that is still being tested in a few countries. It uses the regular rise and fall of the sea, which is caused by the pull of the moon, to drive underwater turbines. Because tides are completely predictable, tidal power could one day become a very reliable source of electricity, although the equipment must survive salt water, strong currents and storms.

The biggest challenge facing renewable energy is storage. Because the sun does not always shine and the wind does not always blow, power grids need a way to save extra electricity and release it later. Lithium ion batteries, similar to those found in mobile phones and electric cars, are now being installed in huge banks that can store energy for several hours. Another method, called pumped hydro storage, uses spare electricity to pump water uphill into a reservoir and then lets it flow back down through turbines when power is needed.

Developing countries face their own set of problems when adopting clean energy. Many villages are far away from the national grid, and building long power lines to reach them is very costly. In these places, small solar systems with a battery can provide light for homes, power for mobile phones and electricity for water pumps. International organisations and private companies are working together to make these systems cheaper and easier to repair.

Despite these challenges, the shift towards renewable energy is gathering speed. Many countries have set targets to reach net zero carbon emissions by the middle of this century. Already over twenty five percent of global electricity comes from renewables, and this figure keeps climbing. The industry has also created millions of jobs in manufacturing, installation and maintenance.

To sum up, clean energy gives us a practical route to a healthier and safer world. Each renewable source has advantages and disadvantages, and none of them can do everything on its own. By combining them, investing in storage and modernising the grid, the world can reduce its dependence on fossil fuels and protect the planet for future generations.
